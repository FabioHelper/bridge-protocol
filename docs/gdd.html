A 4–8 player social deduction game set across one night shift in a Global Network Operations Centre. Nobody dies. People just stop having credentials — and the building keeps a record of everyone who was ever standing anywhere.

Design pillars

Four rules that settle arguments. If a feature does not serve one of them, it does not ship.

The building remembers

Every accusation must be checkable against a record. Suspicion in this game is evidentiary, not vibes.

Horror is procedural

No monsters. The dread comes from process — a badge that stops working, a ticket queue that will not close, a ceiling that keeps dropping.

The night gets worse

Every system escalates on one clock. Nothing about hour four should feel like hour one.

Doubt must survive

Information is always partial. Any mechanic that makes the rogue obvious is a bug, however satisfying it feels.

The fiction

It is 03:00 in a Global NOC. Six operators are on the floor covering the third shift. One of them has privileged access they are not supposed to have, and has spent the last hour quietly issuing logic bombs against production.

The thematic pivot that makes this game its own thing: the antagonist is non-lethal. The Rogue Admin does not kill anyone. They revoke credentials. A deprovisioned operator is still standing there — badge dead, terminal locked, watching. The org chart quietly shrinks. That is more unsettling than a body, and it is the entire reason the game's vocabulary works: nobody is murdered, tickets are closed, incidents are resolved, the shift ends.

Everything is named from inside the job. You do not "do a task" — you close an incident. You do not "call a meeting" — you convene an Emergency Bridge. You do not "win" — operations normalize.

Core loop

ClearWork your queue. Each commit restores SLA and writes a badge record.
ContainFaults appear. Drop your queue and run to the counter-console, or bleed out.
NoticeSomeone goes dark. Who was on that side of the floor?
ConveneRead the access log against what people claim. Vote or skip.
ConvergeQueues clear. Hold the bridge console together while the rogue drains it.

Loop length is roughly 8–14 minutes. The loop is designed so that the work (clearing) and the social (convening) feed each other: clearing generates the badge records that the bridge argues over, and the bridge decides who is still around to clear.

The four acts

One shift clock drives drain, the SLA ceiling, the colour grade, scanline density and the audio bed. There is no separate "difficulty" system — the clock is the difficulty system, and the player reads it on the wall.

The load-bearing idea

The ceiling, not the drain, is what makes the meter mean something. Repairs restore SLA, but never above the current act's cap. What the night costs you, it keeps. By CONTAINMENT a perfect team is playing with 58 points of headroom, so a single un-contained abend is suddenly a 74-second death sentence rather than an inconvenience.

SLA economy

Version 2 shipped with an inert meter. The audit is worth recording because it is the kind of failure that hides behind working code: every system functioned, and the game had no tension.

What was wrong

Idle drain of 0.10/s against 20 tasks averaging 17 SLA restore each meant 340 points of restore chasing a 100-point bar. The meter sat pinned at 100% for the entire shift. The central tension device was decorative.

The fix was structural rather than numeric — a falling ceiling converts surplus restore from "wasted overflow" into "the thing that keeps you alive later". Restore values were left generous on purpose: the crew should feel productive. The pressure comes from the ceiling descending underneath them.

SourceValueNotes
Idle drain0.14 → 0.78/sAct-driven
Abend fault+2.60/sLoud. 38s to kill from full. Cleared by one LPAR repair
Logic bomb+0.75/sSilent — no banner unless a NOC Analyst is on shift
Task commit+12 to +22Capped by the act ceiling
Hotpatchdrain = 0SysAdmin power, 9 seconds

The logic bomb is the most interesting object in this table. It is quiet, cheap and slow, and the only crew member who can see its source is the NOC Analyst. A bomb planted in NOMINAL and left alone is nearly invisible; the same bomb in CONTAINMENT is lethal. It rewards a rogue who plans ahead and punishes a crew who never rostered an analyst.

Information economy

This is the heart of the design, and the thing version 2 was missing entirely. A social deduction game is an information game; without a substrate, voting is a coin flip. Three systems supply it.

Limited vision

You always see the building — you work here, you know the floor plan. You do not always see the people. Operators fade out beyond 8.6 tiles or behind any opaque geometry, with a 2.2-tile falloff so contacts resolve gradually rather than popping. This single rule creates every ambiguity the bridge later argues over.

The access log

The building's badge reader is the deduction engine, and it is thematically free — every real NOC has one. Four record types:

RecordWritten whenReveals
BADGEAn operator lingers at a console (6s cooldown)Name, console, time
COMMITA task is completedName, console, time
REVOKECredentials are revokedPlace and time — never the name
FAULTA non-silent sabotage firesFault type and time

The revocation record is the load-bearing one. It is UNSIGNED: it tells the floor where and when a badge died, but not who did it. Deduction is the act of cross-referencing that place and time against everyone else's badge trail. That is a genuine forensic procedure, and it is the game.

Testimony

Bots speak from a witness memory recorded every 1.4 seconds — who they actually had line of sight to, where that person was standing, and when. At the bridge each bot emits one statement. A sighting only becomes testimony if the subject was within 5.5 tiles of the revocation, the witness within 7, and both within 4.5 seconds of it.

At most two operators may come forward. This is a deliberate ceiling, not a performance limit: a floor that unanimously fingers the same person is a cutscene. Remaining crew fall back to honest, boring alibis — which is exactly what most people in a real incident have.

The rogue lies on a schedule: 70% of the time it names a console it never badged (a falsifiable lie you can catch by reading the log), 30% of the time it tells the literal truth about where it was, which is safer and weaker because being somewhere earlier proves nothing about where you went next.

Worked example — unscripted output from a playtest

Revocation logged at Rack SYS-01. The actual rogue was DELTA.

DELTA"I was on Routing Console B. Pull the log if you want." ← the rogue, telling the truth
GAMMA"I had eyes on EPSILON by Rack SYS-01 right when it went dark." ← honest, and wrong
EPSILON"I was nowhere near Rack SYS-01. I cannot help you." ← flatly contradicts GAMMA
BETA"I had eyes on DELTA by Rack SYS-01 right when it went dark." ← correct

Two eyewitnesses naming different people, one of them denying it outright, and a rogue whose alibi the log will confirm. The player has to reason that a corroborated alibi at 03:41 says nothing about 03:44. None of this was scripted — it is what the three windows produce when a killing happens near a crowd.

Roles & powers

Every role carries passive modifiers and one active power on Q. The passives shape how you play the whole shift; the power is a decision you make three or four times a night. Powers are previewed on the selection screen so the choice is informed.

Design nuance

The Rogue Admin keeps their cover role's power and should be seen using it. A rogue who never hotpatches is a rogue who reads as a rogue. This converts the role system from flavour into active camouflage, and gives the rogue something honest and useful to do in public.

The Rogue Admin

The rogue's toolkit is built around plausible deniability rather than raw damage. Every tool is something a legitimate admin might conceivably have caused.

ToolCDEffectCounter
Trigger Abend26s+2.6 SLA/s, loud alarm, screen shakeOne LPAR repair
Plant Logic Bomb42s+0.75 SLA/s, no alarmTwo alert acknowledgements
Corrupt Zones34sLocks 3 consoles for 38sWait, or a DBA rollback
Seal Bulkheads30sBoth doors impassable for 14sWait. Splits the floor in two
Spoof False Alert38sFull alarm, zero damageNothing — it wastes the crew's time
Purge Access Logs55sTruncates the last 9 recordsNothing — but the purge itself is logged

Spoof and Purge are the two that make the rogue a character rather than a damage source. Spoof is pure misdirection — a real-looking alarm that costs nothing, sending the crew running to a repair that was never needed and manufacturing an excuse for you to be anywhere. Purge is the direct counter to the audit trail, and it is deliberately a double-edged tool: it destroys the evidence against you, but the truncation is itself written into the log, so the crew learns that somebody had something to hide.

Revocation

Requires proximity of 1.7 tiles, clear line of sight, a 22-second cooldown, and the target must be genuinely isolated — bots verify that no third party has eyes on the victim before committing. There is a 14-second opening grace so no shift ends in the first ten seconds. The revocation writes its unsigned record; anyone with line of sight forms real, mechanical suspicion that feeds their vote.

Task design

Tasks are competence fantasies, 8–18 seconds, built from real mainframe operations vocabulary. The hard constraint: every puzzle generates its own answer per attempt, from a rule printed in the modal. You solve them by reading, never by memorising. Version 1's triage form always wanted P1 / Mainframe-Ops — both the first option of each dropdown — which is a puzzle exactly once.

TaskBaseVerbRandomises
Acknowledge Alert11sFind three matching lines in a live scrolling consoleTarget abend code, feed contents, hit count
File Incident Ticket16sRead a triage matrix, set priority and assignment groupSubsystem, impact tier, option order
Cancel Abending Job13sPick the looping job out of a spool displayJob names, which one is faulted, abend code
IPL Recovery Sequence18sExecute four steps in runbook orderWhich four steps, their order, button layout
Verify Routing Table16sRestore three CIDR blocks to their gatewaysBlocks, gateways, VLAN tags, row order
Restore From Tape15sTwo-stage, cross-mapCorruption time, catalogue, seek window and speed

Restore From Tape is the designed outlier. Stage one is in the NOC: read a corruption timestamp and pick the newest backup taken before it. Stage two is in the data centre: stop a moving head inside a restore window. It is the only task that forces you to cross the bulkhead, which is precisely why it exists — it manufactures the long unaccompanied walk that makes alibis meaningful and gives the rogue its best opportunity. It is sorted last in every queue, so the shift's most exposed errand lands in its most dangerous act.

The bridge

Three phases on one authoritative timer. The single-overlay, single-timer construction is deliberate: the prototype's meeting flow had a class of bug where the discussion and voting clocks disagreed.

PhaseLengthShown
DISCUSSION15sFloor statements beside the full access log. Read them against each other
VOTING30sWeighted tally, live. Log stays on screen
REVEAL4.5sWho was deprovisioned, and whether the floor was right

Convening requires standing at the bridge table or being within 4.2 tiles of a locked-out operator with line of sight — you must actually find the body. All living operators are pulled to the table on convene, which resets positional stalemates and gives the next act a clean start. A Problem Manager ignores the 40-second cooldown entirely.

Ties and skip-majorities deprovision nobody, and the reveal says so plainly. Getting it wrong needs to feel like a decision the floor made, not a dice roll.

The finale

Once every queue on the floor is clear, the Master IPL unlocks. Version 2 made this a hold-to-win button in a modal, which is an anticlimax: the game's whole shape is people moving around a floor, and the climax removed the floor.

The finale is now contested space. A charge bar visible to every operator fills while crew stand within 2.4 tiles of the bridge console, at 15 points per operator per second. The rogue standing in the same space drains it at 26 per second.

  • One crew member alone cannot out-charge a present rogue. The crew must arrive together.
  • The rogue must physically commit to the console, in the open, in front of everyone — or remove people first.
  • Crew see the bar fall but cannot see who is draining it unless they can see the person. The last thirty seconds are a scramble to work out who is standing in the room with you.

This turns the endgame into the game's own thesis played at full volume: a crowd, a shared resource, and one person in the room who should not be there.

Being locked out

An eliminated player who has nothing to do is a player who leaves. Deprovisioned operators keep the camera, keep movement, and gain forensic access:

  • They see every operator on the floor, ignoring the vision rule.
  • They read the un-redacted access log — revocations show the actual name, rendered in violet.
  • They can say nothing, vote for nobody, and change nothing.

That is the horror beat and the retention mechanic at once. You know exactly who it is. You watch the floor deliberate. You watch them get it wrong. The banner reads READ-ONLY ACCESS.

AI operators

Five bots fill the floor so a solo or three-player session is still a real game. A seven-state machine, transitions validated against a declared table at boot.

StateBehaviour
PATROLFallback wander when no objective is reachable
PATHBFS route to the next queue item, re-routing when bulkheads change
WORK4–7.5s at a console, then a validated commit through the same reducer players use
FAKE_WORKRogue only. Mimes a work loop at a console it was never assigned
HUNTPursues an isolated target, re-pathing every 0.55s so it routes around geometry
FLEELeaves the scene toward a point at least 7 tiles away
ALIBIRogue re-enters a populated area and looks busy

Bots act only through the same validated reducer as human players — a bot cannot commit a task it was not assigned or revoke someone it is not standing next to. They are honest participants, not privileged scripts. Crew bots weight their vote by mechanical suspicion earned from witnessed revocations and corroborated testimony; rogue bots vote for a crew member.

Art & audio direction

Palette

Amber phosphor on navy — the IBM 3278 terminal, not the green CRT everything else reaches for. Colour is strictly semantic: cyan means nominal, amber means attention, jade means committed, red means breach, violet means forensic. No decorative colour anywhere.

Type

IBM Plex Sans Condensed for facility signage, IBM Plex Mono for every terminal readout. Literally the mainframe vendor's typeface. The corporate face set against the terminal face is the game's thematic tension, rendered in type.

Camera and space

Locked 2:1 isometric orthographic, following with a 0.085 lerp. The bright NOC floor against the near-black data centre is a deliberate contrast: the server hall should read as the dangerous room, and it does so purely through material value.

Audio

A held FM drone under everything, attacked at −60 dB and ramped up so there is no pop at boot. The graph is wired once and never re-patched; a permanently connected parallel bitcrush-and-distortion path sits at zero gain and crossfades up during a fault. Cue throttling is per voice rather than per cue, which is what actually prevents two different cues from being scheduled on the same synth at an identical timestamp.

UX & accessibility

  • Role reveal. A held cinematic card opens every shift: your assignment, your allegiance, your objectives, your power. The rogue's card explains the badge system and tells them their cover role's power is camouflage they should use in public.
  • One interact key. SPACE resolves to exactly one action, chosen by a priority ladder, and the prompt always names it explicitly — CONTAIN · TRIGGER ABEND, not "Interact".
  • Faults name their own cure. The alert banner states which console type contains it and how many repairs remain.
  • Input hygiene. Held keys live in a set, so releasing S never cancels a held W. Keys are lowercased against Caps Lock. Every phase transition blurs the focused element so movement can never be trapped in a text field. INPUT, SELECT, TEXTAREA and contenteditable all suppress movement.
  • Touch. A d-pad appears on coarse pointers; the centre button doubles as interact and as the hold control for charge-based tasks.
  • Reduced motion. prefers-reduced-motion disables the CRT flicker and screen shake and collapses transitions.
  • Focus. Every control has a visible amber focus ring; controls are real buttons with aria-pressed state.

Cut & deferred

Recorded so the reasoning is not relitigated.

  • Voice or text chat. Out of scope for a zero-install page. The access log and generated testimony are the deliberate substitute, and are arguably better for solo play — they give an offline shift a real argument to referee.
  • Multiple rogues. The manifest supports the ratio; the testimony system assumes a single actor for its lie generation. Turning it on needs the lie model reworked so two rogues do not accidentally corroborate each other.
  • Persistent meta-progression. Deliberately shallow — shifts survived, rogue record, fastest IPL, in browser storage. Unlockables would pull the game toward grind and away from a self-contained night.
  • Post-processing bloom. Would need the Three.js addon bundle, which the single-file no-build constraint forbids. Emissive materials, fog and a CSS scanline layer carry the look at 42 draw calls instead.
  • Body positions persisting. Locked-out operators currently stay where they fell, which is correct, but they are not yet obstacles or cover. Deferred.
